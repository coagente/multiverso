# 6. Concurrent Multi-Agent Coordination & Conflict

> Part of the Multiverso research corpus - https://github.com/coagente/multiverso - Cutoff: 2026-08-12

## Why this matters for Multiverso

Multiverso's MVP deliberately restricts itself to the **SELECT regime**: N candidate worlds for the *same* Intent, one admitted to trunk. The phase-D roadmap opens the harder regime — **COMPOSE / SERIALIZE / REPAIR** — where multiple agents do *different* work on the same product concurrently, and the control plane must decide whether their changes can be composed, must be ordered, or must be repaired after one invalidates another's premises. This is no longer a selection problem; it is a **concurrency-control problem over software change authority**, and 2026 produced a burst of directly relevant work: pre-write admission planes ([Claim Plane](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21909), [ATM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00041)), notification-and-repair runtimes ([CoAgent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.15376)), CRDT-based coordination ([CodeCRDT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.18893)), and the first large-scale empirical corpora of agent-PR conflicts ([AgenticFlict](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03551), [AIDev-pop](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.04697)). Understanding exactly what these systems do — and what none of them does — tells us which coordination primitives Multiverso must add at phase D and which it can adopt or adapt.

## State of the art

### 6.1 The measured cost of uncoordinated concurrency

**CooperBench** (Khatua, Zhu et al., Stanford/SAP, arXiv Jan 2026) is the first benchmark of *cooperating* coding agents: 652 tasks over 12 open-source libraries in Python, TypeScript, Go, and Rust, each assigning two agents features that are independently implementable but may conflict without coordination. The headline result: agents achieve on average **30% lower success when working together than when doing both tasks individually** — the authors call it the "curse of coordination," the inverse of human teams. Failures cluster into three modes: communication jammed with vague/ill-timed/inaccurate messages, agents deviating from their own commitments, and agents holding incorrect expectations about others' plans ([CooperBench paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.13295), [cooperbench.com](https://cooperbench.com/)).

**AgenticFlict** (Ogenrwot & Businge, AIware 2026, arXiv Apr 2026) is the first large-scale dataset of *textual* merge conflicts in agent-authored PRs: 142K+ agentic PRs from 59K+ repositories, 107K+ successfully processed through deterministic merge simulation, yielding **29K+ conflicting PRs — a 27.67% conflict rate — and 336K+ fine-grained conflict regions**, with noticeable variation across agents (Copilot, Cursor, Devin, Claude Code, OpenAI Codex). Its acknowledged limitation is central for Multiverso: the pipeline detects **textual conflicts only** — it says nothing about post-merge *logical* incompatibility, i.e., changes that merge cleanly and still break each other ([AgenticFlict, arXiv:2604.03551](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03551); [Zenodo record](https://zenodo.org/records/19396917)).

A companion study on the AIDev-pop dataset (33,596 agent PRs across 2,807 repositories) quantifies how often agents are *actually* concurrent in the wild: **40.2% of repositories had temporally overlapping agent PR pairs, covering 79.4% of all agent PRs** (95.0% within a one-week window). Simulated merges of 747 co-active pairs found **41.7% conflict rates for cross-agent pairs vs 19.8% for same-agent pairs** (non-overlapping 95% CIs); 84.4% of conflicts touched source files and ~42% were structural (modify/delete, add/add) ([AI Agent Pull Requests on GitHub, arXiv:2607.04697](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.04697)). The underlying AIDev corpus itself now aggregates 932K+ agentic PRs from five agents across 116K repositories ([AIDev, arXiv:2602.09185](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09185)).

Takeaway: at fleet scale, concurrent different-work is the *common case*, not the exception; a quarter of agent PRs conflict even textually; and cross-vendor agent mixing roughly doubles conflict probability.

### 6.2 Claim Plane: pre-write admission of versioned ChangeIntents

**Claim Plane** (Maxim Nikolaev, arXiv:2607.21909, July 2026; prototype at [github.com/SkeinRank/claim-plane](https://github.com/SkeinRank/claim-plane)) is the closest thing yet published to Multiverso's phase-D admission layer, and its framing is strikingly aligned: it treats concurrent software change as a **pre-write admission problem** and distinguishes an *integration conflict* (detected after changes exist — a merge conflict is "only one observable symptom") from an **authority conflict** (two active workers claim incompatible rights or premises over a shared software surface, detectable *before* the risky mutation) ([paper PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.21909)).

Mechanics, from the paper:

- **ChangeIntent as the unit of coordination.** An intent is the tuple `I = (id, task, owner, b, O_c, O_q, D, P, T, M, l)`: exact base commit `b`, **committed operations** `O_c`, **contingent operations** `O_q`, declared dependencies `D`, preserve policies `P`, requested acceptance/test evidence `T`, metadata `M`, and a liveness lease `l`. Operations couple an access mode (read/write/extend/delete/rename) with a **typed resource** — exact files, globs, bounded regions, symbols, concepts, contracts, routes, schemas, configuration, or documents.
- **Atomic admission.** An incoming intent is compared against the active set inside one registry transaction, considering exact resource identity, broad scopes, bounded regions, semantic concepts, concept-bound contracts, destructive ops, base revisions, explicit dependencies, and dependency-graph acyclicity. **Unknown overlapping writes fail closed**; broad scope overlap is *not* optimistically treated as independence. Same-file parallelism is allowed only when declared regions are disjoint and later verification confirms actual Git hunks stayed within bounds. The decision vocabulary: `independent`, `compatible_overlap`, `contract_dependency`, `parallel_with_constraint`, `notify_on_change`, `serialize`, `replan_or_reject`.
- **Dynamic Scope / promotion.** Committed mutations reserve write authority immediately; contingent mutations are projected only as read premises. When a worker first attempts a contingent mutation, the control plane **atomically promotes** the narrowest covering path/region into committed authority and **re-runs admission** against the current active set; a rejected promotion leaves the intent unchanged, so "failed speculation cannot silently expand existing authority."
- **Dependency invalidation.** Dependencies are consumer-to-producer edges; when a producer changes a resource a consumer relied on, the consumer becomes **stale**, and staleness propagates transitively downstream until affected work is amended and re-admitted — "avoiding a global lock while still making stale premises explicit and enforceable."
- **Deterministic enforcement.** Brokered execution (a trusted proxy performs file ops), one active writer lease per worktree, an OS-level lock under Git's common directory, **monotonic fencing tokens** attached to broker instances and evidence (a superseded writer fails closed even if still alive), compare-and-swap Git-tree provenance chains, and integration from **immutable frozen worker snapshots** with persisted patch bytes applied producer-first — explicitly closing a time-of-check/time-of-use gap between verification and integration.
- **Evaluation is deliberately preliminary**: six CooperBench pairs, four arms. Naive parallel passed 2/6; static Claim Plane 6/6 but by serializing all six; dynamic scope 3/6 with initial serialization reduced to 3/6 pairs, **7 successful just-in-time promotions and 2 fail-closed undeclared-mutation blocks**; always-serial 4/6. The author explicitly disclaims comparative significance and pre-registers a 30-pair frozen-plan follow-up.
- The stated philosophy is directly quotable for Multiverso: "the model may propose; the control plane decides what authority actually exists" — probabilistic planning separated from deterministic authority, with a proposed three-level escalation (deterministic rules → learned semantic-dependency model → frontier supervisor for low-confidence cases only).

The nearest neighbor, **ATM** (Eagl Huang, arXiv:2607.00041, June 2026), independently formulates governed shared mutation as pre-write admission with semantic atoms, bounded regions, a CID broker, fail-closed routing, and a **neutral steward** that applies writes instead of the proposing agents; its evaluation is a 12-scenario deterministic design matrix plus adopter case studies, claiming feasibility and auditability but no comparative superiority ([ATM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00041)). Claim Plane's own related-work table positions the two as "independent points in a rapidly emerging design space."

### 6.3 CoAgent: concurrency control that turns advisory

**CoAgent** (Lyu, Zhang, Wu, Wei, Chen — Shanghai Jiao Tong; arXiv:2606.15376, June 2026, ATC 2026) attacks the same problem from the runtime side, and its diagnosis of why classical concurrency control fails for agents is the best-articulated in the literature ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.15376), [HTML full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.15376)):

1. **Long transactions**: agent "transactions" span minutes-to-hours of LLM inference; under two-phase locking this measured **0.81 deadlocks per trial** and degraded speedup to 1.04× over serial.
2. **Broad, opaque read sets**: agents list whole repositories and scan entire namespaces, so lock sets and OCC validation sets explode; OCC measured **0.95 aborts per trial, 0.93× (slower than serial), 1.83× token cost** from full re-execution on abort.
3. **Live state cannot be forked**: a `kubectl apply` executes immediately with side effects — neither OCC's buffered install nor 2PL's rollback is implementable against production systems.

CoAgent's core insight is that the LLM inside each agent supplies a capability classical transactions lack: it can **judge whether a conflicting write actually invalidates its plan, and repair exactly the operations that depended on it**. Control therefore "turns advisory: the runtime informs, the agent repairs." The **MTPO protocol** (Monotonic Trajectory Pre-Order) fixes a serialization order σ at launch; every read is *order-filtered* (returns the value the reader's serial position should see); writes apply speculatively in place with per-object write trajectories maintained in σ order (late writes handled by the Thomas write rule, mechanical undo-and-reorder via **pre-registered saga-style inverse operations**, or blocking for non-reversible ops like sending email). When a lower-ranked agent's write touches an object a higher-ranked agent already read, the framework delivers a **one-way invalidation notification** carrying the refreshed value at the reader's next inference; the agent judges relevance and patches only affected pending operations. Correctness is proved as *notified serializability*: at quiescence, live state matches the σ serial order.

Results on ten contended workloads (WorkBench office automation + AIOpsLab live-Kubernetes incidents): **≈95% of serial correctness at 1.4× speedup and 1.15× token cost**, versus naive parallel passing only ~13% of trials; with online tool synthesis on AIOpsLab, 63/71 tasks passed vs 45/71 for a bash baseline at 0.80× time and 0.86× cost. The residual ~5% failures come from **agents misjudging notification relevance** — a number Multiverso should treat as the current floor for un-verified LLM repair.

A formal companion, **Verified Detection and Prevention of Concurrency Anomalies in Multi-Agent LLM Systems** (Sajjad Khan, arXiv:2606.17182, June 2026), formalizes four agent-specific anomalies — stale-generation, phantom-tool, causal-cascade, tool-effect reordering — as structural analogues of classical isolation anomalies, verifies preventive runtimes in TLA+/Verus (274 obligations), and found a silent lost-update bug in ByteDance's deer-flow and tool-effect reordering in LangGraph's ToolNode ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.17182)).

### 6.4 CodeCRDT: structural convergence is not semantic compatibility

**CodeCRDT** (Sergey Pugachev, arXiv:2510.18893, Oct 2025) coordinates agents through **observation of shared CRDT state** rather than message passing: agents monitor a shared document with observable updates and deterministic convergence, skip work peers already did, and generate concurrently lock-free. Across **600 trials** (6 tasks × 50 runs per mode) it reports **100% convergence with zero merge failures** — and, critically, **5–10% semantic conflict rates**: structurally valid merges that are logically conflicting, because "CRDTs guarantee structural convergence but not semantic correctness." Performance was task-dependent: up to 21.1% speedup on some tasks, up to 39.4% *slowdown* on others ([CodeCRDT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.18893)). This is the cleanest experimental demonstration of the gap AgenticFlict cannot see: even with a merge function that *never* fails textually, one in ten to one in twenty concurrent agent edits is wrong after composition.

### 6.5 The classical concurrency-control lens

The 2026 systems map cleanly onto forty-year-old database mechanisms, and the mapping clarifies design space:

- **Pessimistic locking / claims**: Claim Plane and ATM are essentially **hierarchical intention locks with typed, multi-granularity resources** (file → region → symbol → contract), plus leases and fencing tokens straight out of distributed-systems practice. The cost is what it always was: serialization of anything that *might* conflict, i.e., lost parallelism from conservative declarations (Claim Plane's dynamic scope exists precisely to defer that cost).
- **Optimistic concurrency control** (Kung & Robinson, ACM TODS 1981) is what fixed best-of-N-then-merge does implicitly: run to completion, validate (merge + test) at commit, abort on conflict. CoAgent's measurements show why pure OCC is brutal for agents — validation-time abort discards hours of inference (1.83× token cost) ([Kung & Robinson](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/319566.319567); [CoAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.15376)).
- **MVCC** (Bernstein & Goodman's survey, ACM Computing Surveys 1981) is what Git worktrees already give the data plane: each agent reads a consistent snapshot at its base commit. What MVCC does *not* give is write-write reconciliation — exactly the gap the admission planes fill ([Bernstein & Goodman](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/356842.356846)).
- **Transactional memory** (Herlihy & Moss, ISCA 1993) contributed the abort-and-retry-with-speculation vocabulary; CoAgent's speculative in-place writes with compensations are closer to **sagas** (long-lived transactions with semantic inverses) than to TM, precisely because agent work is too expensive to retry wholesale ([Herlihy & Moss](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/165123.165164)).
- The genuinely new element, absent from all classical CC: the transaction itself (the agent) can be **asked whether a conflict matters and to repair selectively** — CoAgent's advisory control — and the scheduler can price that repair in tokens.

### 6.6 Why OT/CRDT collaborative editing does not transfer directly

Real-time collaborative editing solved a different problem. **Operational transformation** (Ellis & Gibbs, SIGMOD 1989) and **CRDTs** (Shapiro, Preguiça, Baquero, Zawirski, SSS 2011; production implementations like [Yjs](https://github.com/yjs/yjs)) guarantee that concurrent *character-level* edits converge to an identical replica state ([Ellis & Gibbs](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/67544.66963); [Shapiro et al.](https://inria.hal.science/inria-00609399)). Three mismatches with agent-scale composition:

1. **Convergence ≠ intent preservation.** Even in rich-text editing, naive CRDT merges destroy user intent (the motivation for Ink & Switch's [Peritext](https://www.inkandswitch.com/essay/peritext/)); in code the unit of intent is a contract or invariant spanning many files, and CodeCRDT's 5–10% semantic conflict rate is the empirical price.
2. **Granularity and latency.** OT/CRDT assume high-frequency, fine-grained, low-cost operations by humans watching each other type. Agents emit large, batched, expensive diffs from divergent plans; the useful conflict unit is the *premise*, not the character.
3. **No notion of validity.** Editing frameworks have no place to attach compilation, tests, or evidence; convergence is the *whole* correctness criterion. Patch-theory VCSs like [Pijul](https://pijul.org), which model patch commutation, sit closer to the compose problem but still operate below the semantic layer.

The correct import from this literature is narrower: CRDT-style **observable shared state** is a cheap coordination substrate (CodeCRDT's agents skipping duplicated work; grite's converging signed event logs), not a merge semantics for code.

### 6.7 Industrial practice, 2025–2026: fleets stepping on each other

- **Anthropic's C-compiler experiment** (Nicholas Carlini, Feb 2026): 16 parallel Claude Opus 4.6 agents, ~2,000 Claude Code sessions, ~$20K, produced a 100K-line C compiler in Rust that builds Linux 6.9 on three architectures and passes 99% of GCC's torture tests. Coordination was **Git plus lock files**: an agent claims a task by writing a file into `current_tasks/`; Git's synchronization forces a second claimant to pick another task; agents pull, merge ("merge conflicts are frequent, but Claude is smart enough to figure that out"), push, release. The observed failure mode is instructive: on monolithic tasks "every agent would hit the same bug, fix that bug, and then overwrite each other's changes" — resolved only by *externally partitioning* the work (using GCC as a reference to split by file) ([Building a C compiler with a team of parallel Claudes](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler)). This is claim-based scheduling with no admission checks, no typed scopes, and no invalidation — and it visibly hit the walls those mechanisms exist for.
- **grite / "Before the Pull Request"** (Dipankar Sarkar, arXiv:2606.19616, June 2026) mined pre-PR coordination events from an append-only signed event log stored inside Git, documenting conflicting edits, lock starvation, redundant rediscovery, and race-to-close among autonomous agents; with the shared substrate, duplicate/conflicting work reportedly dropped from 78% to 0% and useful throughput more than tripled ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19616)).
- **Practitioner playbooks** converged during 2026 on the same few strategies: git-worktree isolation per agent (shared object DB, separate index), serializing raw git operations to avoid metadata corruption, and **serialized merge queues** — merge one PR, run verification, then merge the next ([Augment Code guide](https://www.augmentcode.com/guides/git-worktrees-parallel-ai-agent-execution)). Vendor guidance also recommends "overlap zone registries" for hotspot files (auth, migrations, shared config, API contracts) with mandatory review, frozen base snapshots, and post-merge behavioral verification, citing 50–100 minutes of verification versus 4–8 hours of post-deployment debugging for a 10-PR batch — but publishes no measured conflict rates ([Autonoma, Apr 2026](https://getautonoma.com/blog/parallel-ai-agent-prs)).
- **Orchestration research**: CAID (Geng & Neubig, arXiv:2603.21489) shows centralized delegation + isolated worktrees + integrated-test consolidation beats single agents by 25.6% on PaperBench and 14.7% on Commit0; cohesion-aware task partitioning (arXiv:2606.00953) frames decomposition as graph partitioning over structural dependencies — both reduce conflicts by *planning*, upstream of where Claim Plane/CoAgent enforce ([CAID](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21489); [When Parallelism Pays Off](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00953)).

## Implications for Multiverso design

**1. Adopt the authority-conflict framing; make ChangeIntent claims a first-class extension of the existing Intent object.** Claim Plane's committed/contingent split with atomic scope promotion is the right admission model and is philosophically identical to Multiverso's "deterministic control plane over probabilistic proposers." Multiverso should not reinvent it — it should *subsume* it: at phase D, a versioned Intent grows `O_c/O_q` typed-resource claims and declared dependencies, admitted before any world begins mutating. Crucially, Multiverso claims at the **Intent level, not the world level**: all N candidate worlds of one Intent share that Intent's claim, which is a case none of the surveyed systems handles (they assume one worker per intent).

**2. Evidence is Multiverso's answer to the semantic-conflict gap — make "compose verification" a budgeted oracle.** The two hard numbers to internalize: AgenticFlict's 27.67% is *textual only*, and CodeCRDT shows **5–10% of cleanly-merged concurrent agent work is semantically wrong**. No surveyed system verifies composition with evidence; Claim Plane verifies *containment* (hunks within declared regions), CoAgent verifies *serializability*, CodeCRDT delegates to an un-audited LLM arbiter. Multiverso's COMPOSE decision must therefore mint a **composite world** (the integrated state), run oracles against it, and bind receipts to *that* state — treating post-merge testing not as CI hygiene but as the decision-gating evidence for admission. This is a genuine differentiator.

**3. Extend invalidation from plans/dependencies to *evidence freshness*.** CoAgent invalidates an agent's plan; Claim Plane invalidates a dependency edge and marks consumers stale. Neither invalidates *test results*. Multiverso's evidence plane already has the right primitive — receipts bound to exact world state with freshness — so phase D's rule falls out naturally: **when an admitted producer intent changes a resource in a consumer world's read set, every receipt of that consumer whose oracle transitively touched the resource loses freshness**, and the scheduler must decide whether re-verification, repair, or serialization is the cheapest path to admission. This unifies Claim Plane's staleness propagation and CoAgent's notifications under evidence semantics.

**4. Make REPAIR a scheduler decision, not an agent reflex — and verify it.** CoAgent's ~5% misjudged-notification failure rate is the measured cost of trusting the LLM's own conflict judgment. Multiverso should route CoAgent-style notifications *through the evidence-aware scheduler*: repair is a bid competing for budget against re-generation and serialization, chosen by expected value (probability repair succeeds × cost of repair vs cost of serializing the smaller intent), and a repaired world must re-earn fresh receipts before admission. Under Multiverso's fixed-budget research question, SERIALIZE-vs-COMPOSE-vs-REPAIR becomes precisely the kind of allocation decision the scheduler exists to make.

**5. Steal the enforcement details.** Claim Plane's fencing tokens on writer leases, OS-level worktree locks, CAS Git-tree provenance chains, and integration from immutable frozen snapshots (closing the verify/integrate TOCTOU gap) map directly onto Multiverso's execution and trust planes and should be adopted nearly verbatim — with the noted limitation that the reference implementation is **single-host** (SQLite lease); Multiverso's control plane needs a network-authoritative claim registry.

**6. Use the knowledge plane where Claim Plane proposes a learned model.** Claim Plane's roadmap escalates from deterministic rules to a *learned* semantic-dependency model. Multiverso's optional SCIP/Glean knowledge plane can provide many of those edges **deterministically** (symbol references, call graphs, contract usage), reserving learned/frontier judgment for genuinely ambiguous overlap — a cheaper and more auditable ladder.

**7. Expect serialization to dominate early, and measure it.** Every honest evaluation in this space shows conservatism winning on safety by giving up parallelism (Claim Plane static arm: 6/6 but fully serialized; merge queues in industry). Phase-D metrics should therefore report *admitted-parallelism ratio* and *promotion success rate* alongside false-admission rate, or the system will quietly degenerate into a serial merge queue with extra steps.

## Open questions

1. **Claim vocabulary for semantics**: files/regions/symbols are enforceable; "concepts" and "contracts" (Claim Plane's richer types) are only as good as their extraction. What fraction of real cross-intent conflicts (the CodeCRDT 5–10%) is even *expressible* as a claimable resource?
2. **Compose-verification cost model**: oracles against the composed state can cost as much as all constituent worlds combined. Can pairwise-interaction test selection (change-impact analysis over the knowledge plane) make COMPOSE evidence sub-additive?
3. **N×M interaction**: with N worlds per intent and M concurrent intents, which pairs of worlds ever need cross-intent evaluation? Selecting per-intent first then composing winners is cheap but can select mutually incompatible winners; no literature addresses this.
4. **Fairness and starvation under claims**: grite reports lock starvation among agents; Claim Plane's leases have no priority/aging policy. What is the right scheduling discipline when intents have heterogeneous budgets and value?
5. **Cross-agent heterogeneity**: the 41.7% vs 19.8% cross- vs intra-agent conflict gap ([AIDev-pop](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.04697)) suggests conflict priors should condition on agent identity — should admission be stricter for heterogeneous fleets?
6. **Repair verification depth**: after a CoAgent-style selective repair, how much evidence must be regenerated? Full re-run is safe but destroys the savings; incremental receipt invalidation needs a sound dependency over-approximation.
7. **Distributed claim registry semantics**: Claim Plane is single-host by design; ATM's steward is centralized. What consistency does a multi-host claim plane need (linearizable admission? leases with fencing over an eventually-consistent substrate)?

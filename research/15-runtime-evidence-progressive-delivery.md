# 15. Post-Admission Runtime Evidence: Canary Analysis, Progressive Delivery, and Staged Admission

> Part of the Multiverso research corpus - https://github.com/coagente/multiverso - Cutoff: 2026-08-12

## Why this matters for Multiverso

Industry's actual, deployed mechanism for catching false admissions is not the merge queue — it is the canary. Every hyperscaler and a whole product category (progressive delivery) already run a *statistical judge* over baseline-vs-canary production metrics and automatically halt or roll back releases: Kayenta in Spinnaker ([Netflix Tech Blog](https://netflixtechblog.com/automated-canary-analysis-at-netflix-with-kayenta-3260bc7acc69)), Argo Rollouts AnalysisRuns ([Argo docs](https://argoproj.github.io/argo-rollouts/features/analysis/)), Harness Continuous Verification ([Harness docs](https://developer.harness.io/docs/continuous-delivery/verify/verify-deployments-with-the-verify-step/)), Flagger ([Flagger docs](https://docs.flagger.app/)), and feature-flag guardians like LaunchDarkly's guarded rollouts ([LaunchDarkly docs](https://launchdarkly.com/docs/home/releases/guarded-rollouts)). This matters to Multiverso three ways:

1. **Baseline validity.** Chapter 9's headline metric — false-admission rate (FAR) — will be discounted by any reviewer who notes that production canarying already catches a share of bad merges cheaply downstream. FAR must be quoted *net of what a canary tier would have caught*, or the claimed safety improvement is partially double-counted against industry's existing safety net.
2. **Design.** Runtime evidence is a natural post-ADMIT rung on Chapter 8's oracle ladder — the most expensive, most authoritative, least replayable rung. A graduated ADMIT (admit-to-canary vs. admit-to-fleet) with signed receipts from production metrics closes the loop Chapter 9 flags as unmeasured (post-integration regressions, open question 6) and extends the decision vocabulary.
3. **Go-to-market.** Canary judges are the existence proof that platform teams accept *statistical, automated, evidence-gated no-go decisions* on the release path — the exact buyer behavior Chapter 10's wedge strategy assumes but never evidences. Teams already pay for Guardian add-ons and staff Spinnaker/Argo pipelines; OpenAI paid $1.1B for an experimentation/feature-flag company ([Bloomberg via Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenAI), [Bloomberg, 2025-09-02](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-09-02/openai-to-buy-product-testing-startup-statsig-for-1-1-billion)).

## State of the art

### Kayenta: the canonical open-source canary judge

Netflix and Google open-sourced Kayenta as Spinnaker's automated canary analysis (ACA) service in April 2018, replacing a manual process in which "each canary meant several hours spent staring at graphs" ([Netflix Tech Blog, 2018-04-10](https://netflixtechblog.com/automated-canary-analysis-at-netflix-with-kayenta-3260bc7acc69)). The methodology is deliberate about experimental control: Netflix runs *three* clusters — production, a **fresh baseline** running the production code (typically 3 instances), and a canary running the change (typically 3 instances) — because comparing a new canary against long-lived production instances yields "unreliable results" ([ibid.](https://netflixtechblog.com/automated-canary-analysis-at-netflix-with-kayenta-3260bc7acc69)). At publication Kayenta was running ~30% of Netflix's production canary judgments, ~200 judgments/day ([ibid.](https://netflixtechblog.com/automated-canary-analysis-at-netflix-with-kayenta-3260bc7acc69)).

The default judge (`NetflixACAJudge-v1.0`) is a four-stage pipeline documented in Spinnaker's docs ([Canary judge, Spinnaker docs](https://spinnaker.io/docs/guides/user/canary/judge/)):

1. **Data validation** — missing data handled per strategy; `mustHaveData: true` turns absent metrics into failures (`NodataFailMetric`), otherwise a neutral `Nodata` classification.
2. **Data cleaning** — optional IQR outlier removal using Tukey fences, default sensitivity 3.0.
3. **Metric classification** — a nonparametric **Mann-Whitney U test** per metric; the judge requires "98% confident there's a real difference before flagging," with a dead zone of ±(0.25 × Hodges-Lehmann estimate) around zero to ignore negligible differences. Outcomes: `Pass`, `High`, `Low`, `Nodata`.
4. **Score computation** — group score = (Pass count / total) × 100, weighted-summed into a final score compared against a `passThreshold` and a `marginalThreshold`; metrics marked `critical: true` force the score to 0 when they deviate beyond configured bounds, and >50% NODATA forces failure ([ibid.](https://spinnaker.io/docs/guides/user/canary/judge/)).

This is, structurally, exactly a Multiverso oracle: evidence inputs (metric time series), a versioned judge, thresholds, and a graded verdict (Pass / Marginal / Fail). Lifecycle note: the standalone `spinnaker/kayenta` repository was **archived on Dec 20, 2025**, with the code now maintained inside the Spinnaker monorepo ([GitHub: spinnaker/kayenta](https://github.com/spinnaker/kayenta)) — the judge lives on, but as commodity infrastructure rather than an actively differentiated product.

### Argo Rollouts: analysis as a first-class Kubernetes resource

Argo Rollouts — part of the CNCF-graduated Argo project (graduated 2022-12-06 with 350+ organizations in production) ([CNCF announcement](https://www.cncf.io/announcements/2022/12/06/the-cloud-native-computing-foundation-announces-argo-has-graduated/)) — reifies canary judgment into CRDs: an **AnalysisTemplate** declares metrics, frequency, and success criteria; an **AnalysisRun** instantiates it and completes as `Successful`, `Failed`, or `Inconclusive` ([Argo Rollouts analysis docs](https://argoproj.github.io/argo-rollouts/features/analysis/)). Analyses run **in the background** during progressive traffic shifting or **inline** as blocking steps between canary weights. Providers include Prometheus, Datadog, New Relic, Wavefront, CloudWatch, Graphite, InfluxDB, SkyWalking, Job/Web probes, plugins — and Kayenta itself as a pluggable judge ([ibid.](https://argoproj.github.io/argo-rollouts/features/analysis/)). Decision mechanics that Multiverso should note: `failureLimit` and `consecutiveSuccessLimit` with defined priority, an explicit **Inconclusive** state that pauses for human intervention (a graded ABSTAIN), **dry-run** metrics that observe without gating (shadow-mode judges), and automatic abort — failed post-promotion analysis in blue-green "switches traffic back to the previous stable ReplicaSet" ([ibid.](https://argoproj.github.io/argo-rollouts/features/analysis/)).

### Flagger, Harness CV, and ML-flavored judges

**Flagger** (CNCF-graduated, part of the Flux family) "automates the release process... gradually shifting traffic to the new version while measuring metrics and running conformance tests," across Istio/Linkerd/Kuma and most ingress controllers plus Gateway API, with metric providers spanning Prometheus, InfluxDB, Datadog, New Relic, CloudWatch, Stackdriver, and Graphite ([Flagger docs](https://docs.flagger.app/)). Its judge is simpler than Kayenta's — threshold checks over a rolling window with a failure-count budget — evidence that even *unsophisticated* statistical gates are widely trusted when embedded in the delivery path.

**Harness Continuous Verification** wraps the judge in ML: "Harness applies machine learning algorithms to every deployment for identifying normal behavior, which allows it to identify and flag anomalies in future deployments," and "automatically triggers a rollback if anomalies are found" ([Harness verify-step docs](https://developer.harness.io/docs/continuous-delivery/verify/verify-deployments-with-the-verify-step/)). Sensitivity is expressed in σ-thresholds (High = 1σ, Medium = 2σ, Low = 3σ) with per-metric overrides, recommended windows of 10 min (logs) to 15 min (APM), analysis types keyed to deployment strategy (Canary, Blue-Green, Rolling, Load, Auto), and a **fail-on-no-analysis** option — the same "no data is a failure" semantics as Kayenta's `NodataFailMetric` ([ibid.](https://developer.harness.io/docs/continuous-delivery/verify/verify-deployments-with-the-verify-step/)).

### Feature-flag platforms: statistical rollback guards as a paid product

**LaunchDarkly guarded rollouts** (the "Release Guardian" capability, sold as a Guardian add-on on Enterprise plans) progressively increase traffic while monitoring chosen metrics; the platform "identifies a regression when **sequential testing** determines that the absolute difference represents a statistically significant negative impact," pauses the rollout, notifies, and — if configured — "rolls back the release" automatically ([LaunchDarkly guarded rollouts docs](https://launchdarkly.com/docs/home/releases/guarded-rollouts)). Two details matter: the statistics are *sequential* (valid under continuous monitoring, unlike naive repeated t-tests), and the verdict is rendered on confidence intervals falling entirely on the "worse" side of the metric's success criterion.

Critically for Multiverso, LaunchDarkly now applies the same machinery to AI: **AgentControl** manages prompts, model settings, and agent configurations at runtime, supports guarded rollouts for model-version upgrades and provider shifts, tracks AI metrics (generation count, token usage, latency, errors, evaluation scores), and runs **online evaluations with LLM judges** invocable through the SDK ([LaunchDarkly AI configs docs](https://launchdarkly.com/docs/home/ai-configs)). This is the closest shipped thing (as of 2026-08) to canary-judging AI-produced changes — but the unit of change is a *config/prompt/model choice*, not an agent-authored code diff.

**Statsig** makes every gated rollout an implicit experiment: "Run simple A/B tests without additional setup using Pulse... treat the users who see the new feature as the 'treatment' group," with scheduled staged rollouts and instant kill switches ([Statsig docs](https://docs.statsig.com/feature-flags/overview)). OpenAI agreed to acquire Statsig for **$1.1B** (announced 2025-09-02) with Statsig's founder becoming a senior applications executive ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-09-02/openai-to-buy-product-testing-startup-statsig-for-1-1-billion), cited via [Wikipedia: OpenAI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenAI)) — a frontier lab buying runtime-evidence infrastructure. **Split** was absorbed into **Harness Feature Management & Experimentation (FME)**, which offers "flags, variations, and canary releases" plus "alerts on feature impact" ([Harness FME docs](https://developer.harness.io/docs/feature-management-experimentation/)).

### Hyperscaler release engineering: staged rollout as an evidence pipeline

**Google.** The SRE Workbook defines canarying as "a partial and time-limited deployment of a change... and its evaluation," compared against a *control*, and codifies judge-design folklore: pick metrics that indicate real user-facing problems, that are **attributable** to the deployment, and use "perhaps no more than a dozen" to avoid false positives eroding operator trust; avoid before/after comparisons (too noisy); a 5% canary population turns a 20%-failure defect into a 1% overall error rate ([Google SRE Workbook: Canarying Releases](https://sre.google/workbook/canarying-releases/)). It also warns against concurrent canaries — an early statement of the *canary-slot contention* problem Multiverso's scheduler would face.

**Meta.** Facebook's move to quasi-continuous push (2016-2017) ships "tens to hundreds" of changes every few hours through a fixed evidence gradient: employees first (with **push-blocking alerts**), then **2% of production**, then 100%, ingesting 1,000+ diffs/day, with Gatekeeper decoupling code push from feature exposure and Flytrap aggregating user-visible anomalies ([Meta Engineering, 2017-08-31](https://engineering.fb.com/2017/08/31/web/rapid-release-at-massive-scale/)).

**Microsoft.** Azure's Safe Deployment Practice framework routes every code *and configuration* change through integration → **canary regions** (two full production regions) → pilot → progressive regional rollout, with "automated policies (not developers)" governing rollout velocity, mandatory health-signal monitoring, extended bake times, and automatic mitigation on degradation ([Mark Russinovich, Azure blog, 2020-02-05](https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/blog/advancing-safe-deployment-practices/)). The judge behind it is **Gandalf** (NSDI '20): a lambda architecture with a real-time engine watching a **one-hour window** before/after each deployment and a batch engine over **30 days** for latent issues; the core model does anomaly detection, then **temporal and spatial correlation** of fault signals against all ongoing rollouts, ensemble ranking to attribute blame, and a Gaussian discriminant classifier to decide impact; on a bad verdict it "makes a 'no-go' decision" that automatically stops the deployment engine. In production 18+ months, it caught 155 critical failures in one 8-month window and achieved **92.4% precision at 100% recall** on data-plane rollouts (no Sev0-2 outages caused by bad rollouts) and 94.9% precision / 99.8% recall on control-plane, monitoring 1,200+ region-level deployments with two misses and two false alarms ([Gandalf, NSDI 2020](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi20/presentation/li)). Gandalf is the strongest existence proof that a *fully automated statistical judge with authority to block releases* can run at cloud scale with tolerable false-alarm rates — and that attribution (which rollout caused this signal?) is the hard part, not detection.

**Netflix (pre-production canaries for clients).** For device code where production canarying is slow, Netflix runs ~50 performance tests per commit and gates on two detectors: anomaly detection at 4σ above the recent 40-run mean, plus **e-divisive changepoint detection** over the last 100 runs; run-triplication with min-value filtering cut false-positive alerts ~90% between March and October 2021 ([Netflix Tech Blog, 2022-01-24](https://netflixtechblog.com/fixing-performance-regressions-before-they-happen-eab2602b86fe)). This is the "shift the canary left" pattern: the same statistics, applied pre-admission — directly reusable as a Multiverso oracle rung.

### Academic lineage and 2025-2026 AI-specific work

- **Sequential testing for ramp-ups**: Zhao, Liu & Deb formalized automated staged rollout with a **sequential probability ratio test (SPRT)** variant for continuous metric monitoring plus an automated ramp-up algorithm deciding when to advance exposure ([arXiv:1905.10493](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10493), 2019) — the statistical ancestor of LaunchDarkly's sequential-testing guardian.
- **RL for rollout policy**: Pritchard, Nagaraju & Fiondella demonstrate multi-objective reinforcement learning to dynamically balance delivery speed against failure downtime in staged rollout ([arXiv:2204.02189](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02189), 2022) — precedent for treating exposure schedules as a *learned budget allocation*, cousin to Multiverso's scheduler thesis.
- **Agents as release decision-makers**: "AI-Augmented CI/CD Pipelines" proposes LLM agents as "policy-bounded co-pilots and progressively as decision makers" for exactly the human decision points that stall pipelines — interpreting flaky tests, choosing rollback strategies, tuning flags, "deciding when to promote a canary" — with a **trust-tier framework for staged autonomy** and DORA-based evaluation ([arXiv:2508.11867](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11867), 2025-08-16). Position/architecture paper, not a shipped system.
- **Identity across the canary window**: ICAN-Deploy observes that mainstream canary controllers (Argo Rollouts, Spinnaker, Flagger) "change the deployed system's cryptographic identity during the canary window," breaking "the agent you certified is still the agent you have" for safety-critical embodied agents, and builds an identity-stable canary middleware verified by TLA+ model checking and N=100 real canary cycles ([arXiv:2605.28097](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.28097), 2026-05-27). Directly relevant: Multiverso's attestations face the same identity-drift problem between ADMIT and fleet-wide promotion.
- **DORA data**: the 2024 DORA report found that a 25% increase in AI adoption was "accompanied by an estimated decrease in delivery throughput by 1.5%, and an estimated reduction in delivery stability by 7.2%" ([Google Cloud blog, 2024](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/devops-sre/announcing-the-2024-dora-report)) — AI-authored change *already* degrades the stability metrics that canary systems monitor. DORA's current model tracks change fail rate ("deployments that require immediate intervention... likely resulting in a rollback... or a 'hotfix'") and failed-deployment recovery time as first-class instability metrics ([dora.dev metrics guide](https://dora.dev/guides/dora-metrics-four-keys/)).

**Gap check:** as of 2026-08-12 I found **no published system that runs canary-style statistical judgment specifically over AI-agent-authored code diffs and feeds the verdict back into the admission decision record.** The nearest neighbors are LaunchDarkly AgentControl (judges AI *configs*, not code), arXiv:2508.11867 (agents *making* canary decisions, not being judged by them), and ICAN-Deploy (identity stability, not statistical judgment).

## Implications for Multiverso design

### 1. A receipt schema for runtime evidence

Runtime evidence differs from every other receipt in the evidence plane: it is about a **deployed artifact digest observed during a traffic epoch**, not about a tree hash, and it is **not replayable** — the observation window is gone forever. The bridge from tree to artifact is SLSA-style provenance: an in-toto statement whose subject is the artifact digest and whose predicate records the source commit and builder ([SLSA v1.0 provenance](https://slsa.dev/spec/v1.0/provenance)). Proposed `RuntimeReceipt` (fields distilled from Kayenta, Argo, LaunchDarkly, and Gandalf above):

```yaml
kind: runtime-canary-verdict
subject:
  artifact_digest: sha256:...            # what actually ran
  provenance_ref: slsa-v1-attestation    # artifact digest -> commit -> world/tree -> intent
  admission_ref: decision/...            # the ADMIT this observation tests
exposure:
  strategy: canary | guarded-flag | ring
  cohort: {selector, traffic_fraction}   # e.g. 2% (Meta tier-2 precedent)
  baseline: {kind: fresh-cluster, size}  # Kayenta fresh-baseline rule, NOT long-lived prod
  window: {start, end, traffic_epoch}
judge:
  name: kayenta.NetflixACAJudge | ld.sequential | gandalf-like
  version: v1.0
  config_digest: sha256:...              # thresholds, metric set, weights
  statistics: {test: mann-whitney-u, confidence: 0.98, correction: sequential}
metrics: [{name, classification: Pass|High|Low|Nodata, critical: bool, effect_size, ci}]
score: {value, pass_threshold, marginal_threshold}
verdict: pass | marginal | fail | inconclusive | nodata
confounds: [concurrent_rollouts, flag_flips_in_window]   # Gandalf's attribution problem, explicit
signature: ...
```

**Freshness semantics.** A test receipt is stale when the tree changes; a runtime receipt is stale when (a) the provenance chain from the currently deployed digest back to the admitted tree breaks — including ICAN-Deploy's failure mode where the canary controller itself mutates identity mid-window ([arXiv:2605.28097](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.28097)); (b) the traffic epoch is no longer representative (SRE Workbook: peak-hours defects invisible off-peak — [sre.google](https://sre.google/workbook/canarying-releases/)); or (c) the `confounds` list is non-empty and unresolved — Gandalf exists precisely because concurrent rollouts make naive attribution wrong ([NSDI '20](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi20/presentation/li)). Nodata must be a first-class verdict with policy-selectable severity, exactly as in Kayenta (`mustHaveData`) and Harness (fail-on-no-analysis).

### 2. Graduated admission: ADMIT-to-canary vs. ADMIT-to-fleet

Extend the decision vocabulary: `ADMIT(target=canary, exposure_budget=e, evidence_debt=[runtime-verdict])` and `ADMIT(target=fleet)`, plus `DEMOTE/REVERT` as an automated decision type triggered by a failing RuntimeReceipt — mirroring Argo's abort and LaunchDarkly's automatic flag rollback. An admission with outstanding evidence debt is trunk-visible (an ordinary Git commit, per the core loop) but attestation-marked *provisional*; the attestation is upgraded when the runtime receipt lands. This matches how every surveyed production system actually behaves — Meta's employee→2%→100% tiers, Azure's canary-region→pilot→regions progression — but none of them records the progression as a chain of signed decisions bound to the change's pre-admission evidence.

**Should the scheduler buy production exposure?** Yes, as the top rung of the oracle ladder, priced in three currencies: (i) **error budget** — the SRE Workbook's arithmetic (5% exposure × 20% failure = 1% user impact) makes cost quantifiable; (ii) **latency** — bake times of minutes (Harness: 10-15 min) to 1,000+ of minutes (>20% of Azure rollouts — [Gandalf](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi20/presentation/li)); (iii) **slot contention** — the Workbook's warning against concurrent canaries means canary slots are a serialized scarce resource, like merge-queue slots. Policy consequence: the scheduler should buy exposure only for candidates whose *residual* uncertainty is concentrated in canary-visible dimensions (latency, error rate, resource use, cross-component interactions — Gandalf's catch categories), and never as a substitute for cheap pre-admission rungs. Kayenta's Marginal band and Argo's Inconclusive give the precedent for a three-way outcome: promote, revert, or extend-observation (buy more of the same rung).

### 3. Experimental-protocol amendment for Chapter 9

Add a **canary-counterfactual tier** to the evaluation harness: for every false admission (admitted ∧ Tier-2 incorrect), deploy the merged trunk in a sandboxed serving harness, replay a workload against admitted-version vs. baseline, and run a Kayenta-class judge (Mann-Whitney U on golden signals: error rate, latency percentiles, saturation, plus crash/exception counts). Classify each false admission as **canary-catchable** or **canary-silent**. Report:

- `FAR_gross` (current metric) and `FAR_net = FAR_gross × (1 − canary_catch_rate)` — the honest headline, since industry would have caught the catchable share anyway;
- **cost-of-late-catch**: for canary-catchable cases, the exposure cost (user-impact minutes, rollback and re-work) versus Multiverso's pre-admission cost to reject the same candidate — this is the economic argument for admission-time rigor even where canaries *would* catch the bug;
- the **canary-silent fraction** — the hypothesis to pre-register is that most SWE-bench-style semantic wrongness (wrong-but-plausible behavior on rare paths) does not move golden signals and is therefore invisible to production judges. If true, this is the sharpest possible justification for Multiverso's existence; if false, Multiverso must reposition toward speed/cost rather than safety. Either result is publishable; no benchmark currently measures it (Chapter 9's post-integration-regression metric is the trunk-side half; this is the production-side half).

DORA framing: change fail rate is defined by *immediate intervention* — rollback or hotfix ([dora.dev](https://dora.dev/guides/dora-metrics-four-keys/)) — i.e., industry's headline instability metric already measures exactly "false admissions caught by the runtime tier." The 2024 finding that AI adoption is associated with a 7.2% stability decrease ([DORA 2024](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/devops-sre/announcing-the-2024-dora-report)) says agent-era false admissions are currently being absorbed by this downstream tier; Multiverso's claim should be stated as *moving detections upstream of exposure*, not as detecting the undetectable.

### 4. Go-to-market note for Chapter 10

The trust objection to Multiverso — "no platform team will let a statistical machine gate the release path" — is empirically false: Kayenta ran 200 automated judgments/day at Netflix in 2018 ([Netflix](https://netflixtechblog.com/automated-canary-analysis-at-netflix-with-kayenta-3260bc7acc69)); Gandalf's no-go decisions are enforced across Azure with release managers moving "from being skeptical about Gandalf decisions to enforcing them" ([NSDI '20](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi20/presentation/li)); LaunchDarkly sells the judge as a paid Enterprise add-on ([docs](https://launchdarkly.com/docs/home/releases/guarded-rollouts)); OpenAI valued the category at $1.1B ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-09-02/openai-to-buy-product-testing-startup-statsig-for-1-1-billion)). The buyer Chapter 10 identifies (platform engineering, 50-500 engineers) is the *same team* that operates Argo Rollouts or Harness today. The wedge is interoperability, not replacement: Multiverso should (a) emit admissions whose attestations progressive-delivery tools can consume as promotion preconditions, and (b) ingest AnalysisRun/Kayenta/guarded-rollout verdicts as RuntimeReceipts closing evidence debt — pitching itself as "the missing upstream half of the canary pipeline you already trust, with a receipt chain that finally connects the merge that caused the regression to the rollback that fixed it." Kayenta's archival into the monorepo (Dec 2025) signals the judge itself is commoditized; the defensible layer is the *evidence chain across admission and runtime*, which nobody ships.

## Open questions

1. **Attribution at agent velocity.** Gandalf's hardest problem — which of many concurrent rollouts caused the fault signal — gets categorically worse when agents admit tens of changes/day into shared services; per-change canarying serializes (slot contention), batched canarying destroys attribution. Does the scheduler need Gandalf-style spatio-temporal correlation as a core competence, or can admission batching be co-designed with canary cohorts?
2. **Statistical power at team scale.** Netflix/Azure judges assume traffic volumes most Multiverso customers lack; at low QPS, Mann-Whitney at 98% confidence over a 15-minute window has near-zero power. What is the minimum-traffic regime where a canary rung is worth buying at all?
3. **Per-author priors.** Should runtime verdicts feed back into scheduler priors keyed by agent/model identity (per-author change-fail-rate), effectively a trust-tier system as proposed in [arXiv:2508.11867](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11867)? This risks feedback loops (agents optimizing against the judge) analogous to Chapter 8's judge-gaming concerns.
4. **Identity through the window.** ICAN-Deploy shows mainstream controllers mutate deployed identity mid-canary ([arXiv:2605.28097](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.28097)); Multiverso attestations that claim "the canary verdict is about the admitted tree" must survive the controller's own churn. Is SLSA provenance per-ReplicaSet sufficient, or does the attestation need ICAN-style frozen capability identity?
5. **Canary-silent taxonomy.** Can the classes of defect invisible to golden-signal judges (semantic wrongness, security, rare-path logic) be enumerated well enough to route *those* classes to expensive pre-admission oracles while deliberately delegating perf/interaction defects to the cheap runtime rung?
